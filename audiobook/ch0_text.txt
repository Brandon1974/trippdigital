PROLOGUE: TUITION
The bus dropped him on 79th Street at 11:47 on a Wednesday. Twenty-two years old, but different. No Glock. No backup. Just a duffel with three changes of clothes, four hundred dollars in twenties, and a head full of shit he watched on YouTube.
He thought he knew the streets because he sold a little weed back in Columbus. Thought he was hard because he never cried when his pops left. Thought dice was easy because he watched a tutorial on sleight of hand.
Chicago laughed at him before he even got off the curb.
The air smelled like burnt rubber and somebody's dinner – neck bones, maybe. A woman in a pink housecoat yelled at a man who wasn't listening. Three kids rode a stolen shopping cart down the sidewalk. Somewhere a dog barked the same three notes.
He walked south. Didn't know why. Just followed the sound of bass coming from a car with no plates.
"Yo, white boy."
He didn't turn. He knew better than to turn. But he stopped.
"You hear me?"
He turned.
A dude leaned against a lamppost. Maybe thirty. Gold chain with a Jesus piece so big it looked like a belt buckle. Behind him, two more sat on milk crates. Between them, a piece of cardboard and a pair of dice.
His heart jumped. This was it. The real thing.
"I ain't white," Bones said. "I'm Puerto Rican."
The dude laughed. "My bad, Puerto Rican. You shoot dice?"
"A little."
"A little? We play for real money here. Not little kid shit."
Bones pulled out his roll. Four hundred. He peeled off a hundred. "This little enough?"
The dude's eyebrows went up. The two on crates sat forward.
"Sit down, Rican."
He sat. Cross-legged on cold concrete. The dice were warm. They'd been thrown a hundred times tonight.
"What's your name?" the dude asked.
"Does it matter?"
"Call me Slick. This my block. My dice. My rules. You win, you keep what you get. You lose, you pay. You cheat, you bleed."
Bones nodded. His throat was dry. He didn't bring water.
"Bet a hunnit," Slick said.
Bones laid the hundred on the cardboard. Picked up the dice. They felt wrong. Heavier on one side. He didn't know enough to know they were loaded. He rolled anyway.
Seven.
Slick's jaw tightened. He paid.
"Again."
Another hundred. Eleven. Bones won again. Now he had six hundred. The men on crates whispered.
"Again," Slick said. Flat this time.
Bones was stupid. Not greedy. Stupid. He bet it all. Six hundred.
Rolled.
Snake eyes.
The cardboard didn't move. The dice stared up like two dead eyes.
Slick smiled. "Pay up."
Bones counted out six hundred. His hands shook. He had four hundred left. He should have walked. His mother's voice – don't stay where you losing – played in his head. But his pride was louder.
"Double or nothing," Bones said. "Four hundred."
Slick tilted his head. "You sure?"
"I'm sure."
Bones picked up the dice. Same weight. Same lie. He shook them. Blew on them like he saw in a movie. Rolled.
Three. Point. He needed three again.
Second roll. Six.
Third roll. Three.
He won. The circle went quiet. Slick counted out eight hundred. His smile was gone.
"You got lucky," Slick said.
"No such thing."
"Now you wanna walk, or you wanna be greedy?"
Bones looked at his stack. Twelve hundred. He could leave. Get a motel. Go to Detroit tomorrow.
Instead, he pushed it all to the center.
"One more," he said.
Slick laughed. Not a happy laugh. A laugh that said you just signed your own paperwork.
"Your roll," Slick said.
Bones picked up the dice. Shook them. Felt the weight. Still couldn't read it. Rolled.
Seven natural.
The two men on crates stood up. One cracked his knuckles. The other pulled a sock with a padlock inside.
Bones didn't run. He was too scared to run.
Slick leaned in. "You think you won? Them dice been shaved since 2019. You rolled a seven 'cause I let you. Now you owe me everything you got plus a lesson."
He snapped his fingers.
The padlock sock hit Bones in the ribs. He folded. Air left his lungs like a prayer. Then another hit. His face met concrete. His teeth clicked. He tasted copper.
They took his money. His duffel. His shoes. Left him in a puddle of his own blood and a pair of wet socks.
He lay there for an hour. A woman stepped over him like he was trash. A kid asked if he was dead. Bones didn't answer.
At 4 AM, he crawled to a bus bench. His ribs screamed. His right eye was swollen shut. He had no ID. No phone. No nothing.
He sat there until sunrise. Watched Chicago wake up. Watched a man sell loosies. Watched a cop drive by without stopping.
"I'ma get a gun," Bones whispered to himself. "And I'ma never lose again."
That was the night Bones died.
And the night Bones was born.
