PROLOGUE: TUITION
The bus dropped him on 79th Street at 11:47 on a Wednesday. Twenty-two years old, but different. No Glock. No backup. Just a duffel with three changes of clothes, four hundred dollars in twenties, and a head full of shit he watched on YouTube.
He thought he knew the streets because he sold a little weed back in Columbus. Thought he was hard because he never cried when his pops left. Thought dice was easy because he watched a tutorial on sleight of hand.
Chicago laughed at him before he even got off the curb.
The air smelled like burnt rubber and somebody's dinner – neck bones, maybe. A woman in a pink housecoat yelled at a man who wasn't listening. Three kids rode a stolen shopping cart down the sidewalk. Somewhere a dog barked the same three notes.
He walked south. Didn't know why. Just followed the sound of bass coming from a car with no plates.
"Yo, white boy."
He didn't turn. He knew better than to turn. But he stopped.
"You hear me?"
He turned.
A dude leaned against a lamppost. Maybe thirty. Gold chain with a Jesus piece so big it looked like a belt buckle. Behind him, two more sat on milk crates. Between them, a piece of cardboard and a pair of dice.
His heart jumped. This was it. The real thing.
"I ain't white," Bones said. "I'm Puerto Rican."
The dude laughed. "My bad, Puerto Rican. You shoot dice?"
"A little."
"A little? We play for real money here. Not little kid shit."
Bones pulled out his roll. Four hundred. He peeled off a hundred. "This little enough?"
The dude's eyebrows went up. The two on crates sat forward.
"Sit down, Rican."
He sat. Cross-legged on cold concrete. The dice were warm. They'd been thrown a hundred times tonight.
"What's your name?" the dude asked.
"Does it matter?"
"Call me Slick. This my block. My dice. My rules. You win, you keep what you get. You lose, you pay. You cheat, you bleed."
Bones nodded. His throat was dry. He didn't bring water.
"Bet a hunnit," Slick said.
Bones laid the hundred on the cardboard. Picked up the dice. They felt wrong. Heavier on one side. He didn't know enough to know they were loaded. He rolled anyway.
Seven.
Slick's jaw tightened. He paid.
"Again."
Another hundred. Eleven. Bones won again. Now he had six hundred. The men on crates whispered.
"Again," Slick said. Flat this time.
Bones was stupid. Not greedy. Stupid. He bet it all. Six hundred.
Rolled.
Snake eyes.
The cardboard didn't move. The dice stared up like two dead eyes.
Slick smiled. "Pay up."
Bones counted out six hundred. His hands shook. He had four hundred left. He should have walked. His mother's voice – don't stay where you losing – played in his head. But his pride was louder.
"Double or nothing," Bones said. "Four hundred."
Slick tilted his head. "You sure?"
"I'm sure."
Bones picked up the dice. Same weight. Same lie. He shook them. Blew on them like he saw in a movie. Rolled.
Three. Point. He needed three again.
Second roll. Six.
Third roll. Three.
He won. The circle went quiet. Slick counted out eight hundred. His smile was gone.
"You got lucky," Slick said.
"No such thing."
"Now you wanna walk, or you wanna be greedy?"
Bones looked at his stack. Twelve hundred. He could leave. Get a motel. Go to Detroit tomorrow.
Instead, he pushed it all to the center.
"One more," he said.
Slick laughed. Not a happy laugh. A laugh that said you just signed your own paperwork.
"Your roll," Slick said.
Bones picked up the dice. Shook them. Felt the weight. Still couldn't read it. Rolled.
Seven natural.
The two men on crates stood up. One cracked his knuckles. The other pulled a sock with a padlock inside.
Bones didn't run. He was too scared to run.
Slick leaned in. "You think you won? Them dice been shaved since 2019. You rolled a seven 'cause I let you. Now you owe me everything you got plus a lesson."
He snapped his fingers.
The padlock sock hit Bones in the ribs. He folded. Air left his lungs like a prayer. Then another hit. His face met concrete. His teeth clicked. He tasted copper.
They took his money. His duffel. His shoes. Left him in a puddle of his own blood and a pair of wet socks.
He lay there for an hour. A woman stepped over him like he was trash. A kid asked if he was dead. Bones didn't answer.
At 4 AM, he crawled to a bus bench. His ribs screamed. His right eye was swollen shut. He had no ID. No phone. No nothing.
He sat there until sunrise. Watched Chicago wake up. Watched a man sell loosies. Watched a cop drive by without stopping.
"I'ma get a gun," Bones whispered to himself. "And I'ma never lose again."
That was the night Bones died.
And the night Bones was born.
CHAPTER ONE: THE LESSON
SOUTH CHICAGO – PRESENT
The Greyhound shuddered to a stop like a dying animal. Bones stepped off onto cracked asphalt that steamed in the heat. 1:17 AM. The air smelled like burnt rubber and somebody's dinner – neck bones, maybe. Same as last time. Different block.
He wore black jeans, scuffed Timbs, and a gray hoodie two sizes too big. In his right waistband, a Glock 19X with a full magazine and one in the chamber. In his left sock, three thousand dollars in rubber-banded hundreds. In his right sock, a spare magazine. In his jacket pocket, the bone dice.
He walked three blocks south. Then east. Then south again. He didn't ask directions. He listened.
Click-clack.
The sound came from an alley between a boarded-up church and a house with a missing roof. A single streetlight flickered like a dying heartbeat. Under it, five men. No – six. One sat on a milk crate holding a paper bag. Another leaned against a stolen shopping cart. The dice were on a piece of plywood balanced on two cinder blocks.
They saw him before he saw them. That's how he knew they weren't amateurs.
The talking stopped. The man on the milk crate – gold chain, Jesus piece, maybe forty – tilted his head.
"That's far enough."
Bones stopped ten feet from the plywood. "I'm here to shoot dice."
"We ain't a tourist attraction."
"I ain't a tourist." Bones pulled out his bone dice. Held them up. The light caught the red dots. "These been to thirty cities. They got history. You got house dice or you wanna see mine?"
The man on the crate – Slick, same as before, but Bones didn't let his face change – stared at the bone dice. Then at Bones' face.
"You look familiar."
"I got one of those faces."
"You from around here?"
"No. I'm from everywhere."
Slick laughed. It was dry. No warmth. "Sit down, everywhere. Five hundred minimum. House takes ten percent. You cheat, we break your throwing hand."
Bones sat on an overturned bucket. The circle closed around him. He felt the weight of their eyes on his waistband. He didn't care. Let them see the bulge. Let them wonder.
He laid five hundred on the plywood. Picked up the house dice. They were warm. Shaved on the six side – he could feel it with his thumb. Same trick, different year.
He rolled anyway.
Seven.
Slick paid. No expression.
Another five hundred. Eleven. Bones won again. The circle started murmuring.
A young dude with a teardrop tattoo on his cheek stepped forward. "Let me check them dice."
Bones handed over his bone dice. Teardrop weighed them. Bit one. Scratched a dot with his nail. "Clean."
"Then sit down," Slick said. But Teardrop didn't sit. He pulled out a roll of hundreds so thick it looked like a brick.
"Three grand. Against the traveler. My dice."
Bones nodded. "Your roll."
Teardrop grabbed the house dice. His hands were steady. He'd done this before. He shook them twice – no more – and let them fly.
Four. Point.
Second roll. Seven-out.
The crowd groaned. Teardrop's face went blank. He reached for his waistband – not for money, for something else.
Bones already had the Glock's grip under his hoodie. Not drawn. Just showing enough black polymer to make the point.
"Pay me," Bones said.
Teardrop looked at Slick. Slick nodded once. Slow.
Teardrop counted out three thousand. Slapped it on the plywood.
Bones didn't touch it. "One more. The whole table. Ten grand. You and me, Slick."
The alley went quiet. A distant siren wailed. Somewhere a dog barked.
Slick studied Bones for a long ten seconds. "You got a name?"
"Bones."
"That ain't a real name."
"It's the only one you get."
Slick picked up the house dice. He didn't roll. He just held them. "One roll. My dice. You lose, you leave those bone dice in the gutter and you never come back to this block."
"And if I win?"
"You walk with twenty grand and a story."
Bones nodded.
Slick rolled. Five. Point.
Bones picked up the same dice. He didn't shake them. He just turned them over in his palm. The shaved six was now facing down. He dropped them.
Five.
The circle didn't breathe. Slick stared at the dice. Then at Bones. Then he laughed – a real laugh, loud enough to echo off the abandoned church.
"You cheated," Slick said.
"You noticed."
"How?"
"Trade secret."
Slick counted out ten thousand. Added it to the pile. Pushed it across the plywood. "Get out of here before I change my mind."
Bones scooped the cash. Stood up. Backed away three steps. Then turned and walked. Not fast. Not slow. Just walked.
He felt Teardrop follow him for two blocks. Then a second set of footsteps. Then a third.
Bones turned a corner. Stopped. Put his hand inside his hoodie.
The footsteps stopped.
He waited thirty seconds. Then walked again.
He made it to the Greyhound station at 3 AM. Counted the money in the bathroom. Thirteen thousand eight hundred. Plus his original three. Almost seventeen thousand.
He sat on a bench. Lit a cigarette. A janitor mopped the floor.
"Where the bus go next?" Bones asked.
"Atlanta. Leaves in an hour."
Bones nodded. Stubbed out the cigarette. Bought a ticket.
The Glock never left his waistband.
But he felt it there.
That's what mattered.
CHAPTER TWO: THE A
ATLANTA
The bus dropped him on Forsyth Street at midnight. Atlanta smelled like fried chicken and broke dreams. Bones had slept four hours in two days. Didn't matter. The dice don't care if you're tired. The dice only care if you're stupid.
He took MARTA to Bankhead. Then walked. Past the liquor stores with the iron bars. Past the church that doubled as a daycare. Past the memorial of candles and teddy bears – somebody got hit last week.
He found the game behind a car wash. 1 AM. Circle of eight. A woman ran the bank. Her name was Queen. She had gold fronts and a switchblade in her ponytail.
"You ain't from here," Queen said.
"I'm from everywhere."
"You got a name or a problem?"
"Bones. And I shoot dice."
She looked at the bone dice in his hand. Nodded. "Sit down. Three hundred minimum. House takes fifteen percent – the Bankhead tax."

BONUS: BONES' RULES OF THE DICE
1. Never trust house dice. But never let them know you know.
2. A weapon is for insurance, not intimidation. If you show it, be ready to use it.
3. Greed is the fastest way to lose everything. Know when to walk.
4. Every city has a different tax. Pay it without complaining.
5. The person who smiles the most is the one who will rob you first.
6. Never roll on an empty stomach. Hunger makes you desperate.
7. Sleep on the bus. It's safer than a motel.
8. Keep a spare magazine in your sock. The first one might jam.
9. Bone dice don't lie. But they don't love you either.
10. There's always another game. There's only one you.
